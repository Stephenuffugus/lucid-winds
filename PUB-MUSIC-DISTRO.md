# PUB-MUSIC-DISTRO, getting the soundtracks out honestly

Researched 27 August 2026. Sources are primary (Suno's own terms and help centre,
distributor help centres, Spotify newsroom, Steamworks, itch.io docs, copyright.gov) unless a
line says SECONDARY.

**Updated 27 August after Stephen's response.** Tier question answered (Pro throughout, never
free), the download deadline turns out to be worse than first reported (the cap is
**retroactive to the whole back catalogue**), the "they take the songs back if I stop paying"
fear is **backwards**, and there is a correction to the licensing plan in the new section
**"What you can actually sell, and what you cannot"**.

**The three sentence version.** One thing is urgent and it is bigger than I first wrote: on
3 September the download cap applies to **every song you have ever made**, so pull the entire
library down before then. Everything is on the paid tier, so everything carries commercial
rights. And "where do these go" is not Spotify: **it is a paid Steam Soundtrack and a
standalone itch.io soundtrack project**, because Spotify's 1,000 stream per track threshold
pays a catalogue this size literally nothing.

# ⏰ THE THING THAT NEEDS DOING THIS WEEK

**Suno's Terms of Service change on 3 September 2026. That is seven days from today.**

Under the new terms, commercial rights no longer attach to *making* a song while
subscribed. They attach to a **download**, and downloads are metered:

> "You may commercially exploit Output solely to the extent it adheres to Suno's Conditions
> of Access and Use as outlined above **provided you have obtained a permitted download of
> that Output in accordance with the download allocations for your applicable service
> tier**... **You may not commercially exploit Output that has not been downloaded by you
> through an approved channel** under these Terms of Service."
> — https://suno.com/terms-september-2026 (revised 2026-08-10, effective 2026-09-03)

The allocations: **Free, 7 lifetime trial downloads with no commercial rights. Pro, 20 per
month. Premier, 60 per month.**

⛔⛔ **And the cap is RETROACTIVE. This is the part that makes the deadline real.** Suno's own
FAQ, which I fetched myself on 2026-08-27:

> "**Download limits apply to all music on Suno starting September 3, including songs created
> before that date.** Your existing library is not going anywhere: every song you've made stays
> fully playable and shareable on Suno."
> — https://help.suno.com/en/articles/13614785

Read that plainly: **on 3 September, pulling down a song you made in June starts costing you
one of your twenty monthly downloads.** Every track in the back catalogue, priced the same as a
brand new one. ⚠️ And on today's position: the page says limits start "starting September 3", from which no
limit today follows. It does not state that in so many words, so read it as a sound inference
rather than a quote.

So the window is not "download the ones you might release". It is **download the entire
library, all of it, before Thursday 3 September**, because after that a 200 song back catalogue
takes ten months of allowance to retrieve.

⚠️ You will read that Premier subscribers working inside Suno Studio have **no** download
limit. Suno's blog does say that, twice. **It is not in the contract.** An adversarial check
of the September terms found no Studio carve out anywhere in the commercial use paragraphs,
and the controlling sentence is: "The number of Downloads you are permitted per month depends
on your service tier, **as set out at https://suno.com/account, which may be updated from time
to time.**" That is a marketing promise resting on a page Suno reserves the right to change.
**Do not build a release plan on unlimited Studio exports.**

And after you cancel, "downloads follow free tier limits", so you cannot go back later and
pull down something you made while paying.

## What to do about it, in order

1. **Before 3 September: log into Suno and download EVERYTHING**, best quality, plus stems.
   Not just the 24 distinct recordings in the games (25 files, but Cartridge Moonwalk is the
   same file in two folders). The whole library, including every song you did not
   ship, every alternate take, every thing you half liked. Metering starts on the 3rd and it
   is retroactive, so **right now a download costs you nothing and next week every one of them
   costs allowance**. Stems are free either way: "All stems from a song are part of that song's single download.
   Downloading a song plus its stems counts once, not per file"
   (https://help.suno.com/en/articles/13614785). This costs an evening and it is the
   difference between owning the catalogue and renting it.
2. **Keep the subscription running into September.** See the caveat below; if a pre 3
   September download turns out not to count, you want the allowance available to re pull
   the handful of tracks you actually release. Cancel after that, not before.
3. ✅ **Tier question: answered.** Pro tier throughout, never free. Every track carries
   commercial rights. See the next section for the one small thing still worth checking.
4. **And while you are in there, generate.** Nothing is metered until the 3rd, and Pro gives
   2,500 credits a month, roughly 500 songs. If there is a game that needs a theme, a bed, a
   loss sting, a menu track, this is the week. Every one you make and download now is one you
   never have to spend a download on later.

⚠️ **The one thing I could not resolve, and it is why step 2 exists.** Whether downloads
obtained **before** 3 September count as "a Download you have obtained" under the new
perpetual clause. Suno's blog says "Songs downloaded from Suno on paid plans remain yours to
use commercially or personally", which reads as yes, but the terms define a Download by
reference to the new tier allocations. No Suno source settles it. Re downloading the release
tracks after the 3rd, inside your monthly allowance, removes the question entirely.

# ✅ THE QUESTION THAT DECIDED EVERYTHING, NOW ANSWERED: PAID TIER THROUGHOUT

**Stephen, 27 August 2026: "I've never used the free plan. Everything I've made has been on
the $10 a month plan."**

That is the Pro tier, and it is the answer that unblocks this entire document. Every track in
the inventory below carries commercial use rights. Nothing has to come off the list.

The rest of this section stays in because it explains **why** that mattered, and because two
of the traps still apply to a paid account.

**If a track had been generated on the free tier, it could never be sold, and subscribing later
does not fix it.**

> "If you are a user of the free or Basic tier of the Service then you covenant and agree
> that you will only use such Outputs for your lawful, personal and non-commercial
> purposes."
> — https://suno.com/terms-september-2026

> "If you are using the free version of Suno (our Basic tier), **we retain ownership of the
> songs you generate**, but you are allowed to use those songs for non-commercial purposes."
> — https://help.suno.com/en/articles/2416769

> "Songs made with Suno's free plan can only be used for non-commercial purposes." Suno
> "may offer retroactive rights in certain cases, but this is not guaranteed and applies
> only to specific songs."
> — https://help.suno.com/en/articles/2425729

On a paid tier the position is good: Suno assigns you all of its right, title and interest
in the output, takes no revenue share ("Suno is allowing you to collect 100% of the
royalties without claiming a share", https://help.suno.com/en/articles/9601985), and under
the new terms the assignment is explicitly "**perpetual and... not affected by... the
expiry, cancellation, downgrade or suspension of your subscription**".

### ⭐ The fear that "if I stop paying, they take the songs back" is backwards

Worth being emphatic about, because it is the opposite of what people assume.

**What you keep on cancelling: everything you downloaded, commercially, forever.**

> "Any assignment made to you under the paragraphs above, and the commercial use rights
> described in this paragraph with respect to a Download you have obtained, are **perpetual
> and are not affected by your exhaustion of your Download allotment, by any later change to
> allotments or pricing, or by the expiry, cancellation, downgrade or suspension of your
> subscription.**"
> — https://suno.com/terms-september-2026

**What you lose on cancelling: the ability to download anything new.** The help centre: "your
library stays yours and all of your songs remain in your account. You can listen and share
them on Suno on the free tier. **However, downloads follow free tier limits.**"

So the nightmare, Suno hikes to $50, you refuse, they own your catalogue, **cannot happen to
anything you have downloaded**. It can only happen to what is still sitting on their server
that you never pulled down. Which is why M1 is M1.

**Even the current terms do not support the fear**, though they are silent rather than
explicit. The March 2026 assignment reads "...generated from Submissions made by you through
the Service **during the term of your paid-tier subscription**". Read the grammar: that phrase
says *when the songs had to be made*, not *how long you get to keep them*. It is a window of
eligibility, not a lease. An assignment is a completed transfer, and the sentence contains no
reversion, no revocation and no "for so long as". A grep of the full March terms for "surviv"
returns **zero hits**, so the protection was absent rather than denied.

⚠️ **And here is the awkward wrinkle: the NEW terms are better than the current ones on exactly
this point.** September deletes the timing phrase from the assignment clause entirely, adds the
perpetual sentence quoted above, **and adds a survival clause that names the right section**:

> "The following provisions shall survive termination: General, **Intellectual Property
> Rights**, Dispute Resolution, Indemnification, Limitation of Liability..."

The assignment paragraph sits under that Intellectual Property Rights heading. So the same
update that caps your downloads also, for the first time, puts your ownership in writing and
makes it survive termination. **The cap is a worse deal. The survivability is a better one.**

⛔ **One thing that is genuinely worth fearing, and it is another argument for M1.** Suno's
termination clause lets them "immediately deactivate or delete your account and all related
information and files in your account". That is about their servers, not your ownership, but it
means **the only copy you truly control is the one on your own disk.** Back the downloads up
somewhere that is not Suno and not one machine.

# THE DISTRIBUTOR POLICIES, AND WHO IS CLOSED

## ⛔ First, two routes that are closed

### Bandcamp is out. It banned AI music in January 2026.

> "Music and audio that is generated wholly or in substantial part by AI is not permitted on
> Bandcamp."
> — https://blog.bandcamp.com/2026/01/13/keeping-bandcamp-human/ (accessed 2026-08-27)

Bandcamp also "reserves the right to remove any music on suspicion of being AI-generated",
and it does not accept distributor deliveries in the normal case. Symphonic lists Bandcamp
under partner restrictions for AI content. There is no legitimate route onto Bandcamp for a
fully AI generated soundtrack. The handoff's option (b) was "Bandcamp plus itch.io"; **half
of it is gone** and the itch half survives, and turns out to be most of the answer.

### TuneCore and CD Baby are out, and not for the reason people think.

TuneCore's gate is not "is a human involved", it is **what the model was trained on**:

> "TuneCore only distributes music created using GenAI models trained on fully licensed
> datasets. Music created using models that rely on unlicensed datasets is not eligible for
> distribution through TuneCore."
> And: "Does this apply if I only use GenAI as part of the creative process? **Yes.** If
> GenAI is used at any point in the creation of a track, the tools involved must rely on
> fully licensed datasets."
> — https://support.tunecore.com/hc/en-us/articles/46914166185236 (updated 2026-08-26)

The only approved tool they name is Google Flow Music. Suno is in active litigation with
Universal and Sony over exactly this question, so current generation Suno output does not
clear that bar.

CD Baby, and therefore all of Downtown (FUGA, CD Baby, Downtown Artist and Label Services),
has the same rule:

> "**Unlicensed AI Content:** Content created wholly or primarily by AI models trained on
> copyright protected sound recordings without a license from the applicable copyright
> owners is prohibited."
> — https://support.cdbaby.com/hc/en-us/articles/41589514049037 (updated 2026-06-16)

⚠️ A lot of blog posts say "TuneCore rejects 100% AI but accepts AI assisted". That is the
wrong axis and it will get a release rejected. The axis is the training data.

### Exactly what to tick, and why it is the honest answer

DistroKid asks two separate questions and they must not be confused.

**Question one, about the audio.** Options are "The lyrics", "The music", "**All of the
audio, everything the listener hears is AI-generated**", "Part of the audio". For these
soundtracks the answer is **All of the audio**. Tick it. It is true.

**Question two, only asked if you tick "all of the audio": is the artist name a human or an
AI persona?** The answer for Sky Wolf Studio is **human**, and this is worth understanding
rather than guessing at, because it is the one with money attached.

Spotify launched AI Persona badges on 2026-08-11, and the consequence is severe:

> "**By default, Spotify will not include AI Personas in any editorial or algorithmic
> recommendations.**"
> — https://newsroom.spotify.com/2026-08-11/ai-persona-badges-transparency/

But the trigger is about **identity, not audio**, and Spotify says so directly in the newsroom
post: "**This badge is about the artist's public identity, not about how the music was made.**"
The same post defines the badge as signalling that "an artist's identity may be AI-generated and
does not represent a real person".

⚠️ **A narrower reading circulates, that the trigger is specifically an AI generated
photorealistic human portrait.** I have seen that wording attributed to Spotify's artist support
page but **could not retrieve the page to confirm it**, and the newsroom text is broader. So
treat the narrow version as unconfirmed and act on the broad one.

Sky Wolf Studio is a real studio, run by a real named person, with a website that predates the
release. That is not an AI persona, and ticking the box would be both inaccurate and expensive.

⭐ **The concrete rule that follows holds under either reading: use the studio logo or the game
key art as the artist image, and never an AI generated human portrait.** Sky Wolf Studio is a
real studio run by a real named person with a website that predates the release, so the identity
is not AI generated under any definition of the badge. That is the defensible position and it
costs nothing.

**So the honest pair is: all of the audio is AI generated, and the artist is a real human
studio.** Both true, both disclosed, nothing hidden. That combination is the entire reason
this is doable at all.

⛔ **And the line we do not cross:** never describe the music as played, performed, composed
by hand, or recorded. Not in the DistroKid metadata, not in the album description, not in a
post. The credit is "generated with Suno, curated and edited by Stephen", and if that
sentence costs a placement, it costs a placement.

### ⚠️ And DistroKid's "yes" has no contractual footing

This is worth understanding before treating it as a guarantee. The permission lives entirely
in a **help centre article**, which Distrokid can rewrite at any time. Their actual Terms of
Service, last updated 1 September 2024, **contain zero mentions of AI** (grepped directly),
and contain this:

> "We reserve the right to terminate these Terms, your Account, and your access to the
> Services at any time without notice."
> — https://distrokid.com/terms/

The help centre article's own closing sentence is the same caveat in miniature: "Please note

**DistroKid's yes is a yes at the front door only.**

⚠️ And one piece of context worth pricing in: **DistroKid sold a majority stake to CVC Capital
Partners in July 2026** (SECONDARY, Digital Music News). A permissive, uncontracted, help
centre only AI stance under new private equity ownership is the least durable arrangement of
the six distributors. That is not a reason to avoid it, it is a reason to **download and keep
your masters**, which is item M1 anyway.

  > — https://www.landr.com/terms-of-service/, accessed 2026-08-27

  A fully AI generated game soundtrack is precisely "solely using generative AI Technologies".

help centre article with no contractual force, while every contract is at will.** That is
another argument for routes 1 and 2, where you are selling directly and nobody can
de list your catalogue on a policy update.

# WHAT YOU WILL AND WILL NOT OWN

This is the part that most writeups skip, and it is the part that decides whether this is a
revenue line or a hobby.

**A commercial use licence from Suno is not a copyright.** Suno says so itself:

> "granting commercial use rights does not guarantee copyright protection!"
> — https://help.suno.com/en/articles/9601665

The US Copyright Office's position, from the Part 2 Copyrightability Report (January 2025,
still the operative policy; there is no 2026 publication):

> "Copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or material where there is
> insufficient human control over the expressive elements."
> "Based on the functioning of current generally available technology, **prompts do not
> alone provide sufficient control**."
> "Human authors are entitled to copyright in their works of authorship that are perceptible
> in AI-generated outputs, as well as the **creative selection, coordination, or arrangement**
> of material in the outputs, or **creative modifications** of the outputs."
> — https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf

The Supreme Court **denied certiorari** in *Thaler v. Perlmutter* on 2 March 2026, No. 25-449
(verified on the Supreme Court's own docket: "Mar 02 2026 Petition DENIED").

⚠️ **I first wrote that this made the rule "settled and unappealable". That was wrong and it
is worth being precise about.** A denial of certiorari carries no precedential weight and
decides nothing on the merits. *Thaler* is **D.C. Circuit precedent only**, and its facts were
a work its applicant asserted had **no human author at all**, which is not the same question
as prompt authored output. The authority that actually governs the Suno case is the Copyright
Office's Part 2 report, which is **an agency position, not a court holding**.

That does not change the practical answer, because the Copyright Office is the body that
grants or refuses registrations. It does mean this is a settled *practice*, not settled law,
and it could move.

### What that means in practice

| What you did | Copyright? | Can you enforce it? |
|---|---|---|
| Prompted Suno, released the output as it came | **No** | You hold a contract right from Suno, not a copyright. You cannot register it, you cannot sue an infringer, you cannot make an exclusivity claim stand |
| Your own words over Suno music | The **lyrics** yes, the music no | Enforce the lyrics only |
| Pulled the stems into a DAW, arranged, edited, added parts | **Partly.** Your arrangement and modifications are protectable, the AI parts are not and must be disclaimed on the application | Thin, and limited to what you actually did |

**The route from "licensed" to "owned" is the stems.** That is not me being clever, it is
literally what the Copyright Office says is protectable: creative selection, coordination,
arrangement, and modification. Suno gives stems to Pro and Premier and does not charge an
extra download for them. If any of this catalogue is worth owning rather than renting, the
work is to pull the stems into a DAW and actually arrange them.

⚠️ **The sharp consequence: Content ID will not work.** YouTube's Content ID eligibility
requires exclusive rights to the material (https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311402),
and Suno's own terms warn that Output "may not be unique across users". You can monetise your
own videos with your own music. You cannot police anyone else's use of it, and you should not
plan around a Content ID claim ever standing. LANDR and Symphonic both exclude AI content from
Content ID outright, which tells you what distributors already expect.

⛔ **Do not build a business model that assumes you can enforce ownership of a Suno track.**

# WHAT YOU CAN ACTUALLY SELL, AND WHAT YOU CANNOT

Added 27 August, in response to: *"you now own the music too... we retain the rights to it, so
it's not for public use, so if they want to put it in a game or a movie then they have to pay
us per song."*

**The first half of that plan is good and you should do it. The second half is a bluff you
cannot back, and the reason to drop it is not that it is unenforceable. It is that closing one
deal would put you personally on the hook for a chain of title that does not exist.**

## ✅ The part that is right, and it is most of it

**Shipping downloadable copies of the music with the games is squarely within your rights.**
Suno's paid tier assigns you whatever it has and takes no cut. You can give the tracks away,
sell them, bundle them, put them on your own player, let people load them into their playlists.
Nothing in this section touches that.

And you arrived at the Steam Soundtrack model on your own without knowing it existed. "With the
game you have it as downloadable copies, you now own the music, you can put it on your
playlists" is **exactly** what a Steam Soundtrack does: real MP3s land in the buyer's Steam
music folder, theirs to keep, and it pays 70% from the first sale. That was already route 1 in
this document and your instinct matched it.

## ⛔ The part that does not work: "pay us to use it in your movie"

You cannot license what you do not own, and on raw generated output you almost certainly do
not own anything.

> "Copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or material where there is
> insufficient human control over the expressive elements."
> "**Prompts alone do not provide sufficient human control to make users of an AI system the
> authors of the output. Prompts essentially function as instructions that convey
> unprotectible ideas.**"
> — [USCO, Copyright and AI Part 2](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf)

And on music specifically, from the Copyright Office's letter to the Mechanical Licensing
Collective:

> "One example of where a musical work would not be protected by copyright is 'when an AI
> technology receives solely a prompt from a human and produces... musical works in
> response.'"
> — [USCO to The MLC](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/USCO-Guidance-Letter-to-The-MLC-Letter-on-AI-Created-Works.pdf)

Suno's own assignment clause is written to be true and empty at the same time. Read the
limiter:

> "Suno hereby assigns to you all of its right, title and interest in and to any Output
> **owned by Suno**... Due to the nature of machine learning, **Suno makes no representation
> or warranty to you that any copyright will vest in any Output.**"

Suno assigns what it has. Where no copyright vests, it has nothing, so it assigns nothing. The
disclaimer in the second sentence is Suno telling you that, politely.

### The reason to drop the sync idea is not the weakness. It is the liability.

If a game studio or a film wants to license a track, they will not just hand you money. They
will ask for **chain of title** (who wrote it, who performed it, who owns master and
publishing), **reps and warranties** that you own what you are licensing, and an **indemnity**
from you if they get sued.

You would be warranting an ownership you do not have. And Suno's terms already make **you**
indemnify **Suno**, expressly including "through your generation or use of any Output", with no
indemnity running back to you. So on a single sync deal you would be the only shock absorber
between a film studio and a model trained on scraped music, with nobody upstream to fall back on.

**One bad deal is worse than a hundred no-deals.** That is the whole argument.

⚠️ And there is already an industry mechanism watching for exactly your pattern. The Copyright
Office instructed the MLC to hold royalties on suspected AI works, and named the trigger:

> "...where a songwriter claims that they created **an extraordinary number of musical works in
> an unusually short time period** or makes affirmative statements that a musical work was
> created by AI, it is appropriate for the collective to conduct a timely investigation into
> the work's copyrightability and **hold any royalties**..."
> — [USCO to The MLC](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/USCO-Guidance-Letter-to-The-MLC-Letter-on-AI-Created-Works.pdf)

Volume plus speed is the flag. A week of cranking is the described shape.

## What survives with no copyright, ranked honestly

| Route | Strength | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Contract / EULA terms** | ⭐⭐⭐ strongest | Copyright preemption (17 USC §301) does not reach subject matter outside §102, and non copyrightable AI output is outside it. So a contract term binds. ⛔ **But it binds only the person who agreed.** Anyone who rips the MP3 out of the game bundle never assented and is not bound |
| **DMCA takedown** | ⭐⭐ works in practice, not in law | Platforms honour notices without adjudicating authorship. But a §512(c)(3) notice requires a good faith statement of authorised ownership, and filing on material you know is uncopyrightable risks §512(f) misrepresentation liability. ⛔ **Do not bluff here** |
| **Trademark** | ⭐ dead for the audio | *Dastar* forecloses using the Lanham Act to police copying of a work's content: it would "create a species of mutant copyright law". **Sky Wolf Studio the brand is protectable. The track is not** |
| **Unfair competition** | ⭐ weak | Survives preemption only in a narrow hot news lane. A soundtrack is not hot news |

**The asymmetry to internalise: contract binds the person who clicked, copyright binds the
world.** You are publishing a public download, so everyone past the first downloader is
unbound.

## ⭐ The route that DOES give you something to sell, and you are the right person for it

This is the part worth your attention, because you are a producer and this is producer work.

The Copyright Office has explicitly blessed the tools:

> "there is no dispute that humans can use tools such as **digital audio workstations,
> sequencers, and arpeggiators** to create a copyright-protected musical work, provided that
> the final work is the product of human authorship."

And named the two doors:

> "Human authors are entitled to copyright in... **the creative selection, coordination, or
> arrangement** of material in the outputs, or **creative modifications** of the outputs."

⛔ **But mixing is not one of the doors, and this is the trap.** The Review Board treats
cleanup and polish as *technical*, not authorial, citing Compendium §909.3(A): "Typical
technical alterations that do not warrant registration include... repairing faded print and
visual content; and sharpening and balancing colors, tint, tone, and the like"
(*Théâtre D'opéra Spatial*, p. 5 n.6). Read across to audio and **EQ, compression,
limiting, loudness normalisation, fades and format conversion are almost certainly technical
alterations that add nothing ownable.** "I mixed it, so it's mine" is the exact argument that
failed twice at the Board.

Two real cases showing where the line falls. ⚠️ Both are **Copyright Review Board decisions**,
not the Part 2 report, and they are linked directly here because an earlier draft of this
section quoted them without a source:

- [Review Board, *SURYAST*, 11 December 2023](https://www.copyright.gov/rulings-filings/review-board/docs/SURYAST.pdf)
- [Review Board, *Théâtre D'opéra Spatial*, 5 September 2023](https://www.copyright.gov/rulings-filings/review-board/docs/Theatre-Dopera-Spatial.pdf)


- **SURYAST**: the applicant supplied his own base photograph, chose the style, and set the
  strength value. **Refused.** "Mr. Sahni did not control where those elements would be placed,
  whether they would appear in the output, and what colors would be applied to them." And in a
  footnote that maps straight onto a reverb knob: "selecting the strength of a visual filter,
  by itself, is not sufficiently creative to be protected."
- **Théâtre D'opéra Spatial**: 624 prompts plus Photoshop. **Refused**, because the applicant
  would not disclaim the AI material. The Board explicitly told him the door was open if he
  did: he "would be able to file a new application and explain why his modifications... rise to
  the level of copyrightable authorship."

**So what actually crosses the line:**

| Crosses it | Does not |
|---|---|
| Writing and performing your own topline or lead over the bed | Prompting |
| Cutting a generated bed into sections and re sequencing to your own arrangement | Regenerating until it sounds good |
| Playing real MIDI parts | Picking a favourite take |
| Combining many stems into an original structure with your own transitions and dynamic scoring for game states | Mixing, mastering, EQ, loudness |

⚠️ Watch the floor: "The selection, coordination, and arrangement of only two or three elements
is not generally sufficient."

⚠️ **And one Suno clause in tension with this strategy.** Their terms say you may "edit,
process, or convert the format of an Output you are otherwise permitted to use **to the extent
such use is incidental**." Heavy re cutting is precisely the work that earns copyright, and
"incidental" arguably points the other way. The clause is ambiguous, undefined and unlitigated,
and I could find no Suno guidance construing it. Flagging it rather than resolving it.

## The wording to actually publish, instead of "all rights reserved"

Drop in as is, on the itch soundtrack page, in the game's credits, and in the Steam soundtrack
description:

```
SOUNDTRACK

These tracks were generated with Suno on a paid plan, then chosen, cut and arranged for the
game by Sky Wolf Studio. They ship with the game as a free download.

Use them in videos, streams and personal projects. No permission needed, no credit required,
though it is always appreciated.

We do not claim copyright in the generated audio itself, and we make no promises about its
legal status. Please do not resell it as a stock music or sample library, and please do not
present it as your own original composition.

Want something written to brief for your project? That we can do. Get in touch.
```

Every line of that does work:

- **It discloses AI**, which satisfies Steam's Content Survey and itch's disclosure field, and
  pre empts anyone who thinks they are exposing you.
- **It claims only the arrangement**, which is the part you may actually own.
- **"We make no promises about its legal status"** mirrors Suno's own disclaimer and blocks
  anyone relying on a claim you did not make.
- **The two asks are requests, not threats**, so nothing is unenforceable, because nothing is
  asserted.
- ⭐ **The last line turns the money into a commission instead of a licence.** You cannot sell
  a licence to something you do not own. You absolutely can sell *your time writing music to a
  brief*, and that is the real business anyway. It is also the one thing on this page nobody
  can take from you.

## Where you can be completely firm

**Sky Wolf Studio** as a trademark. Your game code. Your SVG and pixel art. Your writing. Your
own recorded performances. Every bit of that is ordinary copyright and trademark and every bit
of it is enforceable. The soundtrack is the one asset in the building with a hole in it.

⚠️ I am not a lawyer and this is not legal advice. The doctrine is well settled, but if a real
sync agreement with warranties ever lands on your desk, that specific document is worth an hour
of an entertainment lawyer's time.



# THE RISK, STATED PLAINLY

I am not going to bury this.

**Suno is being sued and it is losing in at least one jurisdiction.**

- **Germany, 31 July 2026.** The Regional Court of Munich I ruled against Suno in
  *GEMA v. Suno* (42 O 763/25), and it found infringement not only in the training but in
  **the outputs themselves**. Cease and desist, stop training on protected works, disclose
  revenue, damages to be determined. Not final, appeal expected.
  (https://www.juve-patent.com/cases/munich-regional-court-stops-suno-using-gema-protected-music/)
  This is the most significant ruling to date because output level liability is now
  legally available somewhere.
- **United States.** Warner settled and licensed in November 2025. BMG signed a licensing
  alliance on 12 August 2026, announced by Suno itself
  (https://suno.com/blog/suno-partnership-bmg). **Universal and Sony have not settled.**

  ⚠️ **The dramatic numbers you will read about this case are not verifiable and I am not
  going to repeat them as fact.** Trade press reports that in May 2026 UMG and Sony moved to
  add 61,026 recordings, pushing statutory exposure past $9 billion, and that summary judgment
  motions are due 8 January 2027. An adversarial check read the actual federal docket
  (CourtListener 68878608, *UMG Recordings, Inc. v. Suno, Inc.*, 1:24-cv-11611, D. Mass.,
  Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV) and found: the case is **live and not terminated**, last known
  filing 26 August 2026; the motion to amend on the docket is **entry #126 filed 19 September
  2025**, not May 2026; and the last scheduling order in the record says dispositive motions
  "must be filed by March 13, 2026", a date that has passed with the case still live.

  **What is verified: the case is alive, Universal and Sony have not settled, and Suno has
  conceded training on copyrighted recordings while arguing fair use. The chronology and the
  dollar figure are secondary reporting only.** That is enough to make the decision, and the
  decision does not improve if the number is $84 million or $9 billion.

**And the asymmetry that matters to you:** Suno's terms contain an indemnity that runs one
way. **You** indemnify **Suno**, expressly including "through your generation or use of any
Output". There is no Suno to user IP indemnity in either the current or the September terms.
If a claim ever lands, it lands on you.

**Suno is adopting watermarking and fingerprinting**, and reserves the right to encode
"the applicable service tier of an Output and whether such Output was a permitted Download"
into the file. ⚠️ **Two sources, not one, and an earlier draft blurred them:** the watermarking
announcement and the phrase "so we can partner more closely with distribution platforms on
combatting fraud and misuse" are from the blog
(https://suno.com/blog/building-the-future-of-music-responsibly, 2026-08-06); the "fingerprint,
watermark, or metadata indicating the applicable service tier" clause is from the September
terms (https://suno.com/terms-september-2026). Read that plainly: distributors will increasingly be able to tell a free tier
or undownloaded track from a licensed one, in the file itself. This is another reason the
tier question above is not academic.

### My honest read of the actual downside

**Verified:** no US court has ordered the takedown of end user distributed Suno tracks, and
I found no reported instance of one being pulled from a DSP because of the litigation.

**My inference, labelled as inference:** if Suno loses at summary judgment, the likely
outcome is damages and an injunction **against Suno**, not a hunt for thousands of small
distributors. The realistic ways this reaches you are (a) distributors and DSPs tightening
policy fast in response to a ruling, which needs no court order, (b) one specific track
turning out to be substantially similar to one specific protected recording, and (c) the old
models being retired so you cannot regenerate anything.

**So the risk is real but it is mostly the risk of the door closing, not of being sued.**
Which argues for doing this soon rather than waiting to see how the case goes.

# THE ROUTES, RANKED, AND THE ARITHMETIC THAT REORDERED THEM

I drafted this section recommending DistroKid first. Then I ran the numbers on what a
streaming release actually pays, and the order changed. Here is the number that did it.

## ⛔ Spotify pays a ten track game soundtrack exactly nothing

> "Tracks must have reached a threshold of at least **1,000 streams in the previous 12
> months** to be included in the recorded music royalty pool calculation."
> "There's also a minimum number of **unique listeners** required for a track to become
> eligible... We don't share this number publicly to prevent further manipulation by bad actors."
> — https://support.spotify.com/us/artists/article/track-monetization-eligibility/

**Every track is gated independently. There is no partial credit and no pooling across an
album.** Spotify's own justification for the policy says tracks with 1 to 1,000 annual
streams generate **$0.03 a month on average**
(https://artists.spotify.com/blog/modernizing-our-royalty-system).

A soundtrack for a $2.99 indie game will see tens to low hundreds of streams per track per
year. Every one of those tracks earns **zero**, and the money is redistributed upward. Even
in the good case where all seven Jimothy tracks clear 1,000 streams, at roughly $0.003 to
$0.005 a stream that is **$20 to $35 a year gross**, against a distributor fee of $24.99 a
year, forever. And the undisclosed unique listener minimum means a soundtrack played
repeatedly by a small devoted audience can clear 1,000 streams and still fail.

**The DSP lane is a net loss on this catalogue size. It fails on arithmetic before it ever
gets near a policy.**

⭐ I fetched that Spotify page myself rather than pass the quote along, because this one number
is what reordered the whole recommendation. Both sentences above are verbatim from
`support.spotify.com/us/artists/article/track-monetization-eligibility/`, 2026-08-27. I did
the same with the Bandcamp ban in the section above, for the same reason: it closes a lane.

That does not make it worthless. It makes it a **presence** decision, not a revenue one, and
presence should be priced accordingly.

## 1. ⭐⭐ Steam Soundtrack, on the game that is already going to Steam

This is the answer and I did not expect it to be.

Steam stopped treating soundtracks as DLC in January 2020; they are their own app type
(https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/application/soundtrackapp, which I re read myself
2026-08-27 rather than take second hand). The change that matters:

⚠️ **Be precise about this, because it is a configuration choice, not a default.** A soundtrack
attached to a released game **as DLC** still requires the buyer to own the base game.
Converting it to a **standalone soundtrack app** is what removes that, and Valve's stated
benefit of converting is exactly: "Users who don't own your game will be able to purchase your
soundtracks." **Take the standalone configuration.**

| | Steam Soundtrack | Spotify and the rest |
|---|---|---|
| When you get paid | 70% of every sale, from sale one | $0 until 1,000 streams per track per year |
| Gatekeeper | **none**, it is self serve | CD Baby bans AI outright, TuneCore refuses it |
| AI policy | allowed, disclose in the Content Survey | allowed, then tagged and suppressed |
| Ongoing cost | none, the $100 Steam Direct fee is long paid | $24.99 a year, forever |
| Audience | people who already bought the game and liked the music | strangers who will never find it |

**The specifics you will need:**

- **MP3 is mandatory.** "WAVs, FLAC, AAC, etc. can also optionally be included, but do not
  remove the requirement for MP3s to be present." The client prefers lossless when present.
- ⛔ **"The Steam Music Player determines play order from the filenames, not the metadata."**
  Zero pad them: `01 - Cartridge Moonwalk.mp3`.
- ID3 tags carry track number, track name, artist name, album name. Fill all four.
- Minimum price is the **$0.99 tier** ⚠️ (that figure is from Steam's general pricing doc, not
  from the soundtrack page, which does not address price at all; confirm it in Steamworks).
  A free soundtrack is awkward on Steam, so $0.99 or bundled with the game is the clean path,
  not $0. Valve notes soundtracks "sell particularly
  well as a component of a bundle including the base game", so a Game plus OST bundle is the
  intended shape.
- Files land in the Steam library music folder, not the game's install directory.

**The AI disclosure is already handled and it went in your favour.** Steam has required
generative AI disclosure through the Content Survey since January 2024. Your soundtrack is
**Pre-Generated** content: "content that ships with your game and is consumed by players that
is created with the help of AI tools during development". Valve reviews AI output "the same
way they evaluate all non-AI content", and the January 2026 rewrite narrowed the requirement
so that AI coding assistants no longer need disclosing at all, only content that ships.

⭐ And the stigma argument is over: AI flagged Steam releases went 10.9% in 2024, to 19.9% in
2025, to **30.8% of 2026 releases so far** (Sulka Haro's dataset, SECONDARY). Nearly a third
of new Steam games carry an AI disclosure. Jimothy's store page already discloses. Adding the
soundtrack changes nothing about how the page reads.

## 2. ⭐ itch.io, as its own soundtrack project, NOT as a file on the game page

Second because it needs no permission either, and it stacks with the first.

⚠️ **A correction to my own first draft, and it matters.** I originally wrote "add the
soundtrack as a priced file on the existing Jimothy page". A second research pass found the
rule that kills it:

> "Currently all HTML5 games on itch.io are set up to **only take payments as donations**."
> — https://itch.io/docs/creators/html5, accessed 2026-08-27

The Jimothy itch page is an HTML project. Every payment on it is a **donation**, not a sale,
whatever price you attach to a file. itch's documented workaround is to set the project's Kind
to Downloadable, which is not something you want to do to a browser game.

**So publish the soundtrack as its own itch project.** itch has a first class
[soundtracks section](https://itch.io/soundtracks) and lists soundtracks among its supported
project kinds alongside tools, comics, books and assets. Price it there, and link to it from
the game page's description and from `links.html`.

itch's **per file minimum pricing** ("any price you set is the minimum price", individual
files carry their own minimums, https://itch.io/docs/creators/pricing) is then genuinely
useful within that soundtrack project: a $3 minimum for the MP3s, a higher tier for FLACs, for
example.

⚠️ **Set the file price correctly before launch.** itch's docs warn that raising a file's
price later can lock existing buyers out of that tier.

**Revenue share is yours to set.** The default suggestion is 10% and the slider runs 0 to 100.
⚠️ The often quoted line "As the seller you decide what percentage itch.io gets from each
transaction" is **not on the pricing doc**; it is from itch's original open revenue sharing
announcement. The current [payments doc](https://itch.io/docs/creators/payments) states it as
"You (the seller) can decide what percentage of your sales will go towards itch.io, from 0% to
100%", with a worked example. Payment processing (~2.9% plus 30 cents) is separate.

⛔ **Choose the direct to you Stripe payout, not "collected by itch.io".** Two reasons: it
matches the studio's Stripe only rule, and itch's payment rails have shown they are a single
point of failure under third party pressure (the July 2025 adult content deindexing was
driven by "scrutiny from our payment processors", and there are reported payout delays).

**Fill in the Generative AI disclosure with Sound ticked.** The field was introduced
2024-11-20 and asks which kinds of output the project contains: Graphics, Sound, Text and
Dialog, Code. It is **mandatory for asset creators** and encouraged for games, and a
standalone soundtrack project sits closer to the asset side. The penalty for not tagging is
concrete: untagged generative AI assets "will no longer be eligible for indexing on our browse
pages". Disclose either way. The cost is a tag; the cost of not disclosing is being unfindable.

## 3. The DSPs, for presence only, and only if you want them

If you want the soundtrack to exist on Spotify and Apple because it makes the studio look
like a studio and puts the game's name in a second index, that is a real reason and $24.99 is
a fair price for it. Just do not call it revenue.

**DistroKid is the only viable distributor** (see the policy section above; CD Baby and
TuneCore are closed). Do **Jumping Jimothy (Original Game Soundtrack)** first and only, and
wait to see what happens before queueing another.

⚠️ **A source conflict I am not going to hide.** One of my two research passes read
DistroKid's help centre and quoted its AI articles verbatim with article IDs and update dates
of 19, 25 and 27 August 2026. The other pass got HTTP 403 from the same help centre, found no
AI provisions in DistroKid's public terms, and concluded the policy was unverified. The
verbatim quotes with live article IDs are the stronger evidence and I have gone with them,
**but read the current Distribution Agreement yourself before you upload**, because a
distributor's terms are the document that actually binds you and neither of us read that one.

## 4. ⛔⛔ YouTube: upload freely, and never enrol the music in Content ID

Two separate things, and the second one is a trap with your name on it.

**Uploading is fine, and the AI disclosure does not apply to you.** YouTube's altered or
synthetic content disclosure fires only on **photorealistic** content that makes a real
person appear to say something they did not, alters footage of a real event, or generates a
realistic scene that did not occur
(https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491). Explicitly exempt, verbatim:
"Production assistance, like using generative AI tools to create or improve a video outline,
script, thumbnail, title, or infographic" and "Gameplay footage from video games". **An AI
generated instrumental soundtrack does not trigger it.**

**Content ID is the trap.** If you opt in through a distributor, the reference fingerprint is
registered under that distributor's CMS partner. Content ID then scans **all** of YouTube,
including your own channel. Your trailer, your devlog, your gameplay capture: all claimed, by
a company name you have never heard of, on your own music.

The mitigation is weak. **You can allowlist individual videos after a claim lands. You cannot
allowlist a whole channel**, and allowlisting is often tied to Partner Programme membership.

And you would probably be rejected anyway. Content ID requires **exclusive** rights, and
YouTube's own ineligibility list names "Sound effects, soundbeds, or production loops" and
puts "royalty free production music libraries typically licensed for use in game, film, TV or
other soundtracks" behind manual review
(https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2605065). A looping game soundtrack is both of
those things, and Suno's own terms warn output "may not be unique across users", which is the
opposite of exclusive.

⛔ **Rule: while you plan to post a single trailer or devlog using your own music, do not
enrol that music in Content ID.** The claims against yourself cost more than the revenue ever
could.

Note also that delivering to YouTube Music auto creates a "Sky Wolf Studio, Topic" channel
built from Art Tracks, which your distributor controls and you do not. That is normal and
harmless, just do not be surprised by it.

## ⛔ 5. Bandcamp and the specialist labels are closed

Bandcamp is covered above. The vinyl and VGM labels (Materia Collective, Laced Records,
iam8bit, Ship to Shore, Black Screen Records) are **curated physical media labels, not
storefronts you can self serve into**. They administer rights on behalf of composers, which
is the business with the strongest institutional reason to refuse AI generated material.
⚠️ None of them publishes an AI policy that I could find, and I could not locate open
submission terms for any of them. Treat them as out of scope rather than as long shots.

## ISRC and UPC

DistroKid issues ISRCs free and automatically, and the ISRC is yours and portable: it
"identifies a recording through its entire life and should remain the same despite changes in
distributor, ownership, file type". UPCs are DistroKid generated and not portable on the base
plan. If you ever want your own ISRCs they are purchasable from https://www.usisrc.org/.

Keep a spreadsheet of ISRC to track from day one. Re-releasing the same recording under a new
ISRC is a policy violation at several distributors.

# WHERE THE DSPs STAND, IF YOU GO ANYWAY

None of them ban AI music outright. What they do is grade it, and the grades differ a lot
more than the headlines suggest.

| Platform | Policy | What it means for a small AI instrumental OST |
|---|---|---|
| **Spotify** | Allowed. Disclosure voluntary and explicitly non punitive | Carried, but see the AI Persona note below |
| **Apple Music** | Allowed. AI Transparency Tags shipped 5 March 2026 and are **becoming mandatory** | Carried, and **no demotion is documented anywhere**, which makes Apple the friendliest of the five |
| **Amazon Music** | ⚠️ **No published AI policy could be found at all** | Nothing to violate, and nothing to rely on |
| **Deezer** | Auto detected, tagged, and **fully excluded** from every discovery surface | Carried, invisible, and eventually deleted |
| **Tidal** | Will **not pay royalties** on wholly AI music, and it is barred from direct to fan | Effectively pointless |

**Spotify.** Disclosure is not a penalty: "It's not about punishing artists who use AI
responsibly or down-ranking tracks for disclosing information about how they were made"
(https://newsroom.spotify.com/2025-09-25/spotify-strengthens-ai-protections/). What gets a
release actually removed is unauthorised vocal impersonation of a real artist (not possible
on an instrumental), being a vehicle for artificial streaming, or infringement. Spam patterns
get **suppressed, not removed**. The "over 75 million spammy tracks" figure everyone quotes is
framed by Spotify as spam removal, not AI removal.

⭐ **The AI Persona badge is narrower than I first wrote, and avoiding it is trivial.**
Spotify's own support page defines an AI Persona as "an artist whose public identity
represents an **AI-generated photorealistic human**", and the newsroom post says outright:
"**This badge is about the artist's public identity, not about how the music was made.**"
The consequence is severe (no editorial or algorithmic recommendations by default), but the
trigger is the **artist image**, not the audio.

**So the rule is concrete: use the studio logo or the game key art as the artist image.
Never an AI generated photorealistic human portrait.** Do that and the mechanism never fires,
no matter how the music was made. That is a cleaner and more actionable answer than the one I
gave earlier in this document, and it comes from Spotify's own definition.

**Apple Music.** Four tag types (Artwork, Track, Composition, Music Video), delivered by the
distributor, and Apple's provider email says they "will be required in any instance where AI
was used to create a material portion of the content, including tracks that are AI platform
generated". ⚠️ I could not confirm this from an Apple hosted page; the only evidence is trade
press quoting Apple's email to providers. **No source says Apple demotes labelled tracks**,
which is a real difference from Spotify and Deezer.

**Deezer is the harsh one, and the reason is worth knowing.** It detects AI automatically, no
self disclosure involved, off "signatures left by generative platforms like **Suno and
Udio**". So the tool you used decides whether you get caught. Flagged music is "systematically
excluded from Flow, recommendations, personalized and editorial playlists", and the tag cannot
be turned off. The scale explains the posture: 90,000 AI tracks a day, "more than 50% of all daily uploads
**at peak in June 2026**", generating between 1 and 3% of total streams
(https://newsroom-deezer.com/2026/07/ai-music-exceeds-50-percent-daily-uploads-deezer/).

⛔ **And Deezer's deletion rule is the one that specifically bites a game soundtrack:** it
removes tracks "that haven't been streamed for 6 months or more". A niche OST that goes quiet
for half a year is subject to removal by policy, not by accident.

**Tidal**, since 29 June 2026, "will not knowingly attribute royalties to music identified as
wholly AI-generated", and such releases are not monetizable and not eligible for direct to fan
sales.

## So what is the actual takedown risk?

For a small, instrumental, disclosed, non impersonating AI soundtrack under a real studio
name, released at ordinary volume:

| Actor | Risk | What actually happens |
|---|---|---|
| Spotify | Low for removal | Suppression at worst. Not impersonation, not spam volume |
| Apple Music | Low | No documented demotion. Supplying the tag is compliance, not penalty |
| Amazon Music | Low | No policy found to violate |
| YouTube Music | Low for the music, **high for self inflicted Content ID damage** | See route 4 above |
| Deezer | **Visibility effectively zero**, removal moderate | Auto tagged, excluded, then caught by the 6 month rule |
| Tidal | High for monetization | Wholly AI means no royalties |
| **Your distributor** | ⛔ **The highest risk by a distance** | CD Baby is a certain rejection, TuneCore a likely one, and both carry account level consequences |

**The honest headline: the DSPs are not the ones who would pull you. A distributor is.** And
the lane fails on economics long before it fails on policy, which is why it is route 3 rather
than route 1.
