HOW TO WORK (Stephen's standing order; it binds this pack)
- No tests. No checks. No validation scripts, no pixel audits, no scripts that prove your own files, no reports about
  reports. The last rounds of work on other games spent most of their time on tests that proved nothing, and he pays
  for every minute of it. The art is the deliverable. Nobody reads a test log.
- Look at each sheet ONCE with your own eyes when it is done, fix what is wrong, move on. Never re-render a thing that
  already reads.
- One assessment, written once. One contact sheet per section. One zip. No progress notes, no plans about plans.
- If something is ambiguous, pick the reading that ships and write one line about it in NOTES.md. Do not stop to ask.

THE ART REFERENCE (the whole brief in one picture)
thumbnail.png, in this folder, also live at https://lucidwinds.com/satellites/tomato-man/thumbnail.png (1024 square).
Look at it for a long time before reading further. What is in it, so we agree on what we are copying:
- A tomato headed figure stands alone on pale, wind ribbed sand. The head is a real tomato: matte, mottled, a little
  bruised, with a green calyx worn like a hat. The eyes are deep hollow sockets, dark, with a white slit of pupil low
  in each, half lidded, looking at nothing. The mouth is a small open gap with two little teeth. The face is not sad
  and not scared. It is sun sick. The heat got to him some time ago.
- The body is a stubby block in a torn red tunic, the hem ripped to points, a yellow sun printed on the chest and a few
  green spots. The arms and legs are long, thin and black, like burnt matchsticks, with long thin fingers. The feet
  are orange rubber boots. He is the only upright thing for miles.
- Around him: small green cactus creatures with faces, half buried in the dunes; ripe tomatoes lying in the sand like
  dropped heads; one enormous magenta tomato on the horizon like a planet; grey pebbles. The light is flat and
  bleached, the colours are sun faded, and there is fine grain over everything, like a 1970s stop motion short shot
  on film.
- The feeling is a children's picture book that is slightly wrong. Funny and sad at once. Deadpan. Nothing jumps out,
  nothing is gory, nothing is cute. A kid looks at it and laughs; an adult looks twice.

THE OLD DIRECTION IS DEAD
The repo carries earlier art notes for this game (ART-NEEDED.md and four "Sun-Baked Vinyl" prompt sheets): a cute,
kid friendly, glossy vinyl toy look with thick navy sticker outlines. Stephen has overruled that. Do not use their

WHAT TOMATO MAN IS
A top down shadow runner, one HTML file, canvas 2D, played in portrait with a floating left thumb joystick and action
buttons on the right. The sun is death. Shade is the only ground you can stand on, and the shade MOVES as the sun
sweeps the sky, so the safe ground slides, stretches and vanishes under you. You cross each level from the bottom to
the exit flag at the top by hopping between shadows: the long shadow of an awning, the round shade of an umbrella
(tilt matters, it only shades well when aimed at the sun), a drifting cloud's shadow you can ride, the moving shadow
of a patrolling vendor cart. Out in the open your exposure meter climbs from pale to pink to red and you burn. In
shade it cools.
Moves: DASH (cross a thin sliver of sun fast; it burns you fast in the open), SHADE (drop a temporary safe circle of
your own), SPF (a few seconds of immunity), ICE (briefly slow the sun's sweep, worlds 3 and up). Hot sand burns even
in shade. Wilting awnings sag and stop shading if you camp. Wind drifts you sideways and re-tilts umbrellas. The Angry
Sun, the last world's boss, lunges across the map with telegraphed dives, and an eclipse opens brief windows of safe
dark.
Collect Aloe (green stars that glow when the sun lights them; a dim one is asleep in shade) for shop money; chain
pickups without burning for a Fresh Streak multiplier. Every level hides three treasures (seashells on the beach
worlds, ice creams in the blaze, gems in the dunes and the eclipse); some are a risky grab. Best times are on screen so
you race yourself. 100 percent a level for bonus aloe; 100 percent a world for a hat you cannot buy.
Content: 26 hand built levels in 5 worlds, each verified solvable by a physics replay simulator, plus Shadow Run
(endless) and a Daily Challenge (same seed for everyone, with streaks). The worlds: Morning Tide (long forgiving
shadows, the teaching world), Midday Blaze (short shadows, hot sand, carts, popsicles, the SPF economy), Tide Pools
(wilting awnings, ice water), Dunes at Dusk (wind drift, a wide raking sun), Eclipse (the Angry Sun and the dark
windows). Named set pieces: The Moat (no natural shade crosses the sand; you drop your own and island hop), High Sun
Gaps (shadows only join when the sun stands straight up), The Long Dark (a blackout maze crossed only while the
eclipse blinks on).
Meta: an aloe shop with 8 tiered upgrades (the top tiers gate behind gold medals), single use boosts, 24 cosmetics; a
character builder ("build your tomato man") that swaps the produce body (tomato, strawberry, orange, lemon, lime,
peach, cherry, bell pepper, eggplant, avocado, heirloom, pickle, golden, sunburn), hats (sun hat, ball cap, tiny
sombrero, lettuce cap, lifeguard visor, umbrella hat, sun crown, plus the five world rewards: Shell Crown, Ice-Cream
Hat, Cool Shades, Gem Crown, Eclipse Halo) and dash trails (ember, cool, aloe, rainbow). Medals (gold, silver,
bronze), stars, checkpoints, a first run coach that teaches each mechanic the moment it first matters, procedural
music per world and synthesised sound, a full local save, settings. Sunbeams, the arcade's currency, pay out on play.
It lives at https://lucidwinds.com/satellites/tomato-man/ in the Sky Wolf Studio arcade, open to players, installable,
offline. Source of truth is the GitHub repo Stephenuffugus/Tomato_Man (the arcade carries a vendored copy). It was
named by Stephen's daughter Penny, who kept calling her sunburnt dad "tomato man". Everything on screen today is drawn
by code: the hero is a red circle with two dots for eyes, awnings are rectangles, the sun is a gradient. An image
loader already exists (ASSET_PATHS: hero body and logo) and every draw call can take a sprite behind an image loaded
guard, with the code drawing kept as the fallback. Art is drop in; nothing you make can break the game.

THE HARD LINES (they bind the art too)
- One HTML file, vanilla JavaScript, canvas 2D, no frameworks, no build step. Art arrives as PNG with alpha for
  anything that sits on the world, JPG or WebP for full bleed plates, SVG for UI marks.
- Phone first, portrait, 360 to 430 px wide. The hero is about 28 px across on screen. Every prop must be identifiable
  at thumbnail size, in motion, on bright sand, while the player is reading the sweep. Silhouette over detail.
- The readability contract is the game: cool shade is safe, warm sun is death, the hero is red, aloe is green,
  currency is yellow. The walk on ground must stay LIGHT so the engine's computed shade (a cool translucent polygon)
  reads as the safe layer on top. Your palette comes from the thumbnail; this contract comes first. Reconcile them in
  the style guide.
- Strange, not scary. No gore, no blood, no horror faces, no body horror. The uncanny comes from deadpan, hollow eyes,
  wrong proportions, bleached light and a figure who is too calm about his situation. A seven year old must be able
  to play it. Never cute vinyl, never sticker outlines.
- Every target 48 px. No real brands, no real people, no other game's characters, no mascot from anywhere else.
- Generated art is never called hand painted. No text in a plate except a wordmark and whatever a row asks for.
- Player facing words use no dashes of any kind and no exclamation marks (the fleet's copy law); keep that in any
  sample copy you write.
- Google Fonts only for type. Never a side over 1600 px except the store sizes. Web files stay small: a plate under
  200 KB, a sprite sheet under 150 KB (quantize if you must).

WHAT THE ENGINE DRAWS (so your footprints fit; the swept shadow is computed from these)
- Hero: a circle, radius about 14 world px, seen from slightly above; the engine draws the face, blush, sweat and the
  hat on top of the body sprite, so deliver the in game body FACELESS and HATLESS, and deliver faces and hats as
  separate layers it can stack. Exposure tints the body pale to pink to red; a baked state sheet is also welcome.
- Casters (the shade makers): rect awnings (footprint w by d, pivot top left), round umbrellas (radius r, pivot at the
  pole; the engine rotates the sprite for tilt), a four post cabana, a billboard, a palm (top down star of fronds), a
  leaning palm, a lifeguard tower, a rock arch, a cliff overhang, a vendor cart that patrols (wheels at the bottom
  edge). The art is the physical object; the engine draws the shadow.
- Pickups and markers at a single point: aloe star (lit and dim), sunbeam, popsicle (dash refill), ice water, SPF
  bottle, shade token, the placed shade pool, checkpoint flag (off and on), exit flag, the three treasures, cloud
  platforms.
- Hazards and weather: hot sand patch, heat shimmer, wind streaks, the Angry Sun and its eclipse.
- Everything else is UI drawn by the page: menus, world cards, the builder, the shop, How to Play, the win card.
Exact pixel sizes per file are in PART 2 (the same footprints as the old manifest, so the loader needs no change).

WHAT IS WRONG TODAY (our read; find the rest)
- Nothing on screen looks like the thumbnail. The hero is a red circle. The worlds are coloured gradients. The sun is
  a radial blur. The only time a player sees the art that sold them is the arcade card.
- The menus are a flat navy page: a How to Play wall with four emoji in rounded squares, then five identical rounded
  rectangles for the worlds with a lock emoji. "Reflective water, wilting shade" is described in words and shown with
  nothing. No beach, no sand, no sun on the front door.
- 87 emoji stand in for icons: the sun, a white circle for shade, a joystick, a dash puff, padlocks, the aloe leaf in
  the wallet, the star and shell progress marks.
- No wordmark (the code asks for art/ui/logo.png and gets a 404), no painted app icon, no world art, no boss face.
- The fun is in the levels and the feel; the look does not yet tell anyone that.

PART 1. THE ASSESSMENT (write it first; it decides the rest)
Play it at the live URL in a phone sized window, as a nine year old who found it on the arcade, then as a player on
day 30 chasing the last gems and the Eclipse Halo. Then write:
  a. ASSESSMENT.md:
     - What already reads and must be kept (the sweep, the dash, the drop shade, the awning tilt puzzle, the treasure
       hunt, the Angry Sun, the builder).
     - What does not read, confuses, or looks unfinished, screen by screen. Be blunt.
     - Anything that breaks a hard line.
     - THE LOOK: name it, define it in a paragraph from the thumbnail, and say how it survives at 28 px on sand (what
       stays: the hollow eyes, the calyx, the black limbs, the orange boots; what goes).
     - THE WORLDS: each of the five as a place in the thumbnail's universe. What grows there, what is half buried,
       what the casters are made of, what the sky does. The thumbnail's cactus creatures, dropped tomatoes and the
       planet sized tomato are yours to spread across the worlds.
     - THE SUN: decide whether the ordinary sun has a face in every world (it is the villain of the whole game, and
       today it is a gradient). The Angry Sun must grow out of the same design.
     - THE FUN: ranked improvements worth building (code, ours). Seeds: the sun watches you (its face turns to follow
       the hero); the shadow of something enormous crossing the map as a moving platform (the planet tomato, a
       passing bird); hats that do a tiny thing (the umbrella hat drops a shade, the ice cream hat melts into a
       puddle); a melt meter instead of a bar; a chase level; a boss per world rather than one; pass the phone races
       on the same level; a share card ("Burned at High Sun Gaps, 14 seconds from the flag"); a daily with a seed
       word to send a friend; the level card as a postcard. Say which three you would ship first and why, and what
       each needs from the art.
     - The three biggest risks to this selling and what you would do about each. One is ours to raise: the strange
       look must read as charming in the FIRST store screenshot, not as a horror game; another is that a top down
       sprite of the thumbnail's hero can lose everything that makes him him.
     - The store read: what a parent and a kid see in the first screenshot, and what the six screenshots show in
       order.
  b. STYLE-GUIDE.md (or a PDF with the same name): the look the game ships with. Palette (hex, from the thumbnail:
     the bone sand, the faded tomato red, the calyx green, the matchstick black, the boot orange, the planet magenta,
     and the cool shade blue the contract needs), light (flat, bleached, grain), surface rules (what a prop is made
     of, how a cutout sits on sand, the contact shadow), type (Google Fonts; something that could be stamped on a
     1970s seed packet is a fair starting point), icon grammar (one colour marks that read at 24 and 48 px), motion
     (what wobbles, what never moves, what the burn looks like), the hero's turnaround, the five worlds' identities.
     Show it on the hero, on one world's play screen mocked over a real capture, and on the world select.

WHAT NOT TO DRAW
- Anything cute, glossy, vinyl, sticker outlined, or that would sit happily in a cereal commercial. If it would not
  belong in the thumbnail, it does not belong in the game.
- Anything gory or genuinely frightening. The hero is unwell, not injured.
- Text inside plates, except the wordmark and anything a row asks for. Store screenshots: we compose those inside your
  frames from the real game.
- Real brands, real people, other games' characters. Sounds (notes about sound go in NOTES.md).
